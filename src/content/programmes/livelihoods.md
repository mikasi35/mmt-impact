## Why this programme exists

The distinction between relief and development is often drawn too sharply. In
practice most households need both, and the useful question is when the balance
should shift. A household that can earn is a household that stops needing the
distribution.

Livelihoods programming is where that shift is engineered. It is also where
development work most often fails quietly, because it is genuinely difficult: the
constraint varies by household, markets are thin, and results take longer than a
funding cycle.

## Finding the binding constraint

Most livelihoods programmes default to training, because training is the easiest
thing to deliver and count. But for a great many households the binding constraint
is not skill. It is capital, or storage, or transport to a market, or the absence
of a buyer willing to commit.

So we assess before we design. Value chain and market analysis establishes where
the loss of value is actually occurring, and support is placed there. Sometimes
that is a skill. Frequently it is a storage facility, an aggregation arrangement or
a route to a buyer.

## Agriculture

Agricultural potential in South Sudan is substantially underused, and
post-harvest loss is a persistent drain on the value that is produced. We work on
production, handling, storage and market linkage together, because improving yield
without improving storage and route to market simply increases the volume that
spoils.

## Honesty about markets

If a market cannot absorb more producers of a given good, training more producers
into it transfers income between households rather than creating it. Where our
assessment finds that, we report it and redesign — including when the alternative
is a less presentable programme.
